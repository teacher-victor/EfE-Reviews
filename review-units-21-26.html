Review: Units 21–26

Units 21.1, 21.6

Using "there is" and "there are"

Use There is for one thing and There are for more than one, to say that something exists. Make it negative with There isn't or There aren't. We often contract "there is" to There's.

There is a hospital. There are three hospitals. There isn't a school. There aren't any schools. There's a park nearby.

There is / There are

PositiveNegative
SingularThere is / There'sThere isn't
PluralThere areThere aren't

We use "any" with plural negatives and questions: "There aren't any schools," "Are there any schools?"

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. one hospital a hospital nearby.
  2. three hospitals three hospitals in this town.
  3. no school here a school in this village.
  4. no stores here (plural) any stores nearby.
  5. a contraction a park across the street.
  6. asking about hotels any hotels in town?
  7. asking about a bank a bank nearby?
  8. two restaurants two restaurants on this street.

Reading practice

Welcome to Maple Town! There is a small hospital on Main Street, and there are two schools nearby. There isn't a train station, but there's a bus stop on every corner. Are there any hotels? Yes, there are three hotels near the park. There isn't a supermarket in the center, but there are two just outside town. There aren't many restaurants, but the few that exist are excellent.

Check your understanding

1. There is a hospital in Maple Town.

2. There is a train station.

3. There are three hotels near the park.

4. There is a supermarket in the center.

5. There are many restaurants in town.

Unit 22.1

Articles

Use a or an for one thing when it isn't specific, or when you mention it for the first time. Use the when the listener already knows which one you mean.

I work in a library. I work in the library on Main Street. She bought a book. The book was expensive. The sun is bright today.

a/an vs. the

ArticleUseExample
a / anfirst mention, not specificI saw a cat.
thealready known, specificThe cat is mine.

We use "the" for things that are unique, like the sun, the moon, or the internet.

Grammar practice: choose a, an, or the

  1. first mention I saw cat in the yard.
  2. already mentioned cat was orange.
  3. a unique thing sun rose early today.
  4. first mention, vowel sound She bought apple.
  5. specific, already known place I work in library on Main Street.
  6. not specific I need pen.
  7. already known Where is pen I gave you?
  8. first mention He has dog.

Reading practice

Yesterday, Mia went to a bookstore near her house. She found a book about gardening. The book had beautiful pictures, so she bought it. On her way home, she saw a cat sitting near a fence. The cat looked hungry, so Mia gave it some food. Later, she read the book she bought. The pictures inside were even better than the cover.

Check your understanding

1. Mia bought a book about cooking.

2. The book had beautiful pictures.

3. Mia saw a dog near a fence.

4. Mia gave the cat some food.

5. The pictures inside the book were worse than the cover.

Unit 22.8

Using "any" and "some"

Use some in positive sentences for an unspecified amount. Use any in questions and negative sentences.

Are there any hotels? There are some hotels. I have some money. I don't have any money.

any vs. some

Sentence typeWordExample
PositivesomeThere are some apples.
QuestionanyAre there any apples?
NegativeanyThere aren't any apples.

We can also use "some" in a question when offering or asking for something: "Would you like some tea?"

Grammar practice: choose any or some

  1. positive statement There are hotels near the station.
  2. question Are there hotels in town?
  3. negative I don't have money left.
  4. positive She bought apples at the market.
  5. question Do you have questions?
  6. negative There isn't milk in the fridge.
  7. positive We need more chairs for the party.
  8. offering something Would you like coffee?

Reading practice

At the grocery store, Leo checks his list. "Do we have any eggs?" his mother asks. "Yes, we have some eggs," he says, "but we don't have any milk." They walk to the dairy section and find some milk. "Are there any apples on sale?" his mother asks. "There are some apples over here," Leo says, pointing. At the checkout, the cashier asks, "Would you like some bags?" "Yes, please," says Leo's mother. "We don't have any with us today."

Check your understanding

1. Leo's family has some eggs.

2. They already have milk at home.

3. There are some apples on sale.

4. The cashier offers them some bags.

5. Leo's mother has her own bags with her.

Unit 23.1

Imperatives

Use the base form of the verb, with no subject, to give a command, instruction, or warning. Add Don't before the verb to make it negative.

Stop! Be careful! Open the door. Don't touch that. Turn left at the corner.

Forming imperatives

TypeFormExample
Positive commandbase verbClose the window.
Negative commandDon't + base verbDon't touch that.
Polite requestPlease + base verbPlease wait here.

Imperatives don't use a subject pronoun — we say "Stop!" not "You stop!"

Grammar practice: complete each command

  1. warn someone about danger careful!
  2. telling someone to stop right there!
  3. negative command touch the stove.
  4. giving a direction left at the light.
  5. polite request sit down.
  6. negative command forget your keys.
  7. instruction the door before you leave.
  8. base verb, no subject quiet in the library.

Reading practice

The lifeguard blows her whistle. "Stop!" she shouts to a boy near the deep end. "Don't swim past the rope," she warns him. "Be careful, everyone!" she calls to the group. A younger child runs near the pool. "Don't run!" the lifeguard says firmly. "Walk, please." Later, she reminds everyone, "Please listen for the whistle. When you hear it, get out of the water immediately." Everyone nods and follows her instructions.

Check your understanding

1. The lifeguard tells the boy to keep swimming past the rope.

2. The lifeguard warns the group to be careful.

3. The younger child is told to run faster.

4. Everyone should get out of the water when they hear the whistle.

5. The lifeguard never gives any instructions.

Units 24.1, 24.7

Joining sentences

Use and to join two similar ideas. Use but to join two contrasting ideas. Add a comma before "but" when it joins two full sentences.

There's a library and a restaurant. There's a hotel, but there isn't a store. I like tea and coffee. She is tired, but she keeps working.

and vs. but

WordUseExample
andjoins similar ideasa library and a restaurant
butjoins contrasting ideasa hotel, but no store

Use a comma before "but" when it joins two full sentences: "There's a hotel, but there isn't a store."

Grammar practice: choose and or but

  1. two similar things There's a park a playground.
  2. a contrast The town is small, it is very friendly.
  3. two similar things I like apples oranges.
  4. a contrast He wanted to come, he was too tired.
  5. two similar things There's a bank a post office.
  6. a contrast The hotel is nice, it is expensive.
  7. two similar things We visited Paris Rome.
  8. a contrast She studied hard, she still failed.

Reading practice

Our town has a lot to offer. There's a library and a museum, and both are free to visit. There's a nice park, but it doesn't have a playground yet. The restaurants are excellent, but they can be expensive. We have a train station and a bus stop, so getting around is easy. The weather is usually sunny, but it can get cold in winter. Overall, it's a great place to live, and most people love it here.

Check your understanding

1. The library and museum are free to visit.

2. The park has a playground.

3. The restaurants are always cheap.

4. The town has both a train station and a bus stop.

5. Most people dislike living in the town.

Units 25.1, 25.4

Using adjectives

An adjective describes a noun. It can come before the noun, or after "to be". Adjectives don't change form for singular or plural nouns.

She is a busy woman. It is a busy town. The town is busy. It is busy.

Where adjectives go

PositionExample
Before the nouna busy town
After to beThe town is busy.

Adjectives never take a plural -s in English: we say "busy towns," not "busies towns."

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. before the noun (busy) She is a woman.
  2. after to be (busy) The town is .
  3. before the noun, plural (busy) They have jobs.
  4. after to be (hot) The soup is .
  5. before the noun (hot) It's a day.
  6. after to be, plural subject (small) The rooms are .
  7. before the noun (small) We live in a house.
  8. adjectives don't change for plural nouns (big) I have three dogs.

Reading practice

The city center is always busy. There are busy streets, busy shops, and busy people everywhere. Ana works in a small office near the busy market. The office is quiet, but the street outside is loud. Her apartment is small, but it's comfortable. On weekends, the city feels less busy, and Ana enjoys the quiet mornings. Her friend says the city is too busy for her, but Ana loves the energy.

Check your understanding

1. The city center is quiet.

2. Ana's office is near the busy market.

3. Ana's apartment is large.

4. The city feels less busy on weekends.

5. Ana's friend loves the city's busy energy as much as Ana does.

Unit 26.1

Using "because"

Use because to give a reason for something. It comes before a full clause — a subject plus a verb, not just a noun.

I live in the countryside because it's beautiful. She is tired because she worked all night. We stayed inside because it was raining. He studies English because he wants to travel.

Using because

StructureExample
result + because + reasonI live here because it's quiet.

"Because" always introduces a reason — a full clause with its own subject and verb, not just a noun.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence with because

  3. giving a reason We stayed inside it was raining.
  4. giving a reason He studies English he wants to travel.
  5. giving a reason They moved their old house was too small.
  6. giving a reason I'm happy it's Friday.
  7. giving a reason She left early she felt sick.
  8. giving a reason We chose this house it has a big garden.

Reading practice

Diego decided to move to a small town because he wanted a quieter life. He chose this house because it has a big garden. His neighbors are friendly because they welcome everyone with open arms. Diego walks to work because the office is close by. He doesn't own a car because he doesn't need one here. On weekends, he visits the market because the fresh food is wonderful. Diego is happy because he made the right choice.

Check your understanding

2. Diego's house has a big garden.

3. Diego drives to work every day.

4. Diego visits the market on weekends.

5. Diego regrets his decision to move.
