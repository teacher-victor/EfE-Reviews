Review: Units 28–35

Units 28.1, 28.6

Using "have"

Use have (I/you/we/they) or has (he/she/it) to talk about possession. Make it negative with do not have (don't have) or does not have (doesn't have).

I have a garage. She has a yard. I do not have a bathtub. They have two cars. He doesn't have a garden.

have / has

SubjectPositiveNegative
I / You / We / Theyhavedon't have
He / She / Ithasdoesn't have

"Have" changes to "has" only for he, she, and it — just like other present simple verbs.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. (I) I a garage.
  2. (she) She a yard.
  3. (they) They two cars.
  4. negative, (I) I a bathtub.
  5. (he) He a garden.
  6. negative, (she) She a pool.
  7. (we) We a big kitchen.
  8. negative, (they) They a basement.

Reading practice

The Alvarez family just bought a new house. They have a large garage and a small garden. Mr. Alvarez has an office upstairs. Mrs. Alvarez has a studio for painting. Their daughter doesn't have her own bathroom, so she shares one with her brother. They don't have a swimming pool, but they have a nice yard for the kids to play in. The house doesn't have a basement, but it has a big attic instead.

Check your understanding

1. The Alvarez family has a large garage.

2. Mrs. Alvarez has an office upstairs.

3. The daughter has her own bathroom.

4. The house has a swimming pool.

5. The house has a big attic.

Unit 29.1

Asking "have" questions

Use Do you have...? (I/you/we/they) or Does she have...? (he/she/it) to ask about possession. After "does," the verb stays as have, not "has."

Do you have a TV? Does she have a car? Do they have a garden? Does he have a bicycle?

Asking about possession

SubjectQuestion formExample
I / You / We / TheyDo + subject + haveDo you have a TV?
He / She / ItDoes + subject + haveDoes she have a car?

After "does," the verb stays as "have," not "has": "Does she have a car?" not "Does she has a car?"

Grammar practice: choose do or does

  1. (you) you have a TV?
  2. (she) she have a car?
  3. (they) they have a garden?
  4. (he) he have a bicycle?
  5. (we) we have enough chairs?
  6. the verb stays as "have" after does Does she a car?
  7. (it) it have four wheels?
  8. (you) you have any questions?

Reading practice

At the electronics store, the salesperson asks customers questions. "Do you have a TV already?" she asks a young couple. "No, we don't," they say. "Does your apartment have cable?" she continues. "Yes, it does," says the woman. Another customer walks in. "Do you have any laptops on sale?" he asks. "Yes, we have several," the salesperson says. "Does this model have a good battery?" he asks, pointing at one. "It does," she answers. "It lasts about ten hours."

Check your understanding

1. The young couple already has a TV.

2. The woman's apartment has cable.

3. The store has laptops on sale.

4. The laptop's battery lasts about ten hours.

5. The salesperson asks no questions.

Units 31.1, 31.4

Countable and uncountable nouns

Countable nouns can be counted and have a plural form, like "eggs." Uncountable nouns cannot be counted and have no plural form, like "rice." Use There are with countable plural nouns and There is with uncountable nouns.

There are four eggs. There is some rice. Are there any eggs? Is there any rice?

Countable vs. uncountable

Noun typeExampleQuestion form
Countable (plural)four eggsAre there any eggs?
Uncountablesome riceIs there any rice?

Uncountable nouns like rice, water, and money never take a plural -s or a number directly in front of them.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. countable, plural four eggs in the fridge.
  2. uncountable some rice in the pot.
  3. countable question any eggs left?
  4. uncountable question any rice left?
  5. uncountable some water on the table.
  6. countable, plural six apples in the bowl.
  7. uncountable We need more sugar.
  8. countable question any chairs in the room?

Reading practice

In the kitchen, Grandma checks the pantry. There are four eggs on the shelf, and there is some flour in a jar. "Is there any rice left?" she asks. "Yes, there is some rice in the cupboard," says her grandson. "Are there any onions?" she continues. "There are three onions," he answers. "Good," she says. "There isn't much sugar, but there is enough for the recipe."

Check your understanding

1. There are four eggs on the shelf.

2. There is no flour in the pantry.

3. There is some rice in the cupboard.

4. There are three onions.

5. There is plenty of sugar.

Unit 32.1

Using "enough" and "many"

Use enough before a noun to mean the right amount. Use too many before a countable plural noun to mean more than needed. Use too much before an uncountable noun.

We have enough eggs. We have too many eggs. There isn't enough time. She has too much homework.

enough / too many / too much

WordNoun typeExample
enoughcountable or uncountableenough eggs / enough time
too manycountable pluraltoo many eggs
too muchuncountabletoo much sugar

"Too many" and "too much" both mean "more than is good," but "too many" is for things you can count, and "too much" is for things you can't.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. the right amount, countable We have eggs for the recipe.
  2. more than needed, countable We have eggs — let's use fewer.
  3. the right amount, uncountable Is there time to finish?
  4. more than needed, uncountable She has homework this week.
  5. the right amount, countable Do we have chairs for everyone?
  6. more than needed, countable There are people in this small room.
  7. more than needed, uncountable He drinks coffee every day.
  8. the right amount, uncountable We don't have milk for the cake.

Reading practice

Before the party, Sam checks the kitchen. "Do we have enough snacks?" he asks his roommate. "I think so," she says, "but we might have too many drinks — nobody will finish them all." Sam checks the chairs. "We don't have enough chairs for everyone," he says. His roommate nods. "And there's too much noise coming from next door already!" They both laugh. "At least we have enough time to fix the chairs before people arrive," Sam says.

Check your understanding

1. Sam thinks they don't have enough snacks.

2. They might have too many drinks.

3. They have enough chairs for everyone.

4. There is too much noise from next door.

5. Sam feels there isn't enough time to fix the chairs.

Unit 34.1

Shopping verbs

Use verbs like own, sell, want, and fit to talk about shopping. "Own" means to possess something, "sell" means to offer it for money, "want" means to desire it, and "fit" means to be the right size.

Ana owns a red hat. Luc sells old clothes. They want new shoes. The hat fits Jane.

Shopping verbs

VerbMeaningExample
ownto possessShe owns three hats.
sellto offer for moneyHe sells shoes.
wantto desireI want a new coat.
fitto be the right sizeThese shoes fit well.

"Fit" describes whether something is the right size — it doesn't mean the same as "like" or "want."

Grammar practice: choose the right verb

  1. (Ana / possess) Ana a red hat.
  2. (Luc / offer for money) Luc old clothes.
  3. (they / desire) They new shoes.
  4. (the hat / right size) The hat Jane.
  5. (I / desire) I a new jacket.
  6. (this shop / offer for money) This shop jewelry.
  7. (she / possess) She two cars.
  8. (these shoes / right size) These shoes perfectly.

Reading practice

At the market, Jane looks for a new coat. "I want something warm," she tells the seller. The seller, Mr. Kim, owns a small clothing stall. "I sell coats from local designers," he says proudly. Jane tries on a green coat. "This fits perfectly!" she says. "How much does it cost?" Mr. Kim smiles. "It's a great choice. My stall sells the best coats in town." Jane happily buys the coat, glad it fits so well.

Check your understanding

1. Jane wants a new coat.

2. Mr. Kim owns a large department store.

3. Mr. Kim sells coats from local designers.

4. The green coat does not fit Jane.

5. Jane buys the coat.

Unit 35.1

Adjective order

When using more than one adjective before a noun, follow a general order: opinion, then size or age, then color. "A lovely green hat" (opinion before color) sounds natural; "a green lovely hat" does not.

This is a lovely green hat. She has a beautiful old house. He bought a small red car. It's a nice warm coat.

Adjective order

OrderTypeExample
1Opinionlovely, nice, beautiful
2Size / Agesmall, old, big
3Colorgreen, red, black

Opinion adjectives (like lovely or nice) usually come before more factual adjectives like size or color.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. opinion + color (lovely) This is a green hat.
  2. correct order (old) She has a beautiful house.
  3. correct order (red) He bought a small car.
  4. opinion comes first (nice) It's a warm coat.
  5. correct order (black) We saw a big dog.
  6. opinion first, then size (lovely) That's a small apartment.
  7. correct order (blue) I bought a nice jacket.
  8. opinion before age (new) She has a beautiful car.

Reading practice

At the antique shop, Mia finds a lovely old lamp. Next to it, there's a beautiful red vase. The shop owner shows her a nice small mirror with a gold frame. "I love this pretty green chair," Mia says, pointing across the room. She also notices a charming little table near the window. "Everything here is wonderful," she tells the owner, admiring a handsome brown desk in the corner.

Check your understanding

1. Mia finds a lovely old lamp.

2. The vase is blue.

3. The mirror is large.

4. Mia likes the green chair.

5. There is a handsome brown desk in the corner.
