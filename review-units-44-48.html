Units 44.1, 44.3, 44.7

"Can," "cannot," and "can't"

Use can plus the base verb to say someone is able to do something. Use cannot or the contraction can't for the negative. "Can" doesn't change for different subjects.

I can ride a bicycle. He can play guitar. I cannot sing jazz songs. I can't sing jazz songs.

can / cannot / can't

FormMeaningExample
canability, positiveI can swim.
cannot / can'tability, negativeI cannot swim. / I can't swim.

"Can" doesn't change for he, she, or it — we say "He can play," not "He cans play."

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. (I / ability) I ride a bicycle.
  2. (he / ability) He play guitar.
  3. (I / inability, formal) I sing jazz songs.
  4. (I / inability, contraction) I sing jazz songs.
  5. (she / ability) She speak French.
  6. (they / inability) They come to the party.
  7. (we / ability) We finish this today.
  8. (it / inability) It fly.

Reading practice

Leo is proud of his skills. "I can ride a bicycle," he tells his friend. "I can also swim pretty well." His friend Mia laughs. "I can't ride a bicycle at all!" she admits. "But I can play the piano." Leo shakes his head. "I can't play any instrument." Their teacher joins the conversation. "Everyone can do something well," she says. "I cannot draw, but I can write stories." Mia smiles. "Maybe we can teach each other."

Check your understanding

1. Leo can ride a bicycle.

2. Mia can ride a bicycle easily.

3. Mia can play the piano.

4. Leo can play an instrument.

5. The teacher can draw well.

Units 45.1, 45.4

Using adverbs

Use an adverb to describe how an action happens. Many adverbs are formed by adding -ly to an adjective. Adverbs usually come after the verb.

I speak quietly. He speaks loudly. She walks slowly. They work carefully.

Forming adverbs

AdjectiveAdverbExample
quietquietlyspeak quietly
loudloudlyspeak loudly
slowslowlywalk slowly

Some adverbs are irregular: "good" becomes "well," not "goodly."

Units 45.7, 45.8

"Good at" and "bad at"

Use good at or bad at plus a gerund (verb + -ing) or a noun to talk about skill level.

She's good at running. I am bad at making cakes. He's good at math. They are bad at cooking.

good at / bad at

PhraseMeaningExample
good atskilledgood at running
bad atnot skilledbad at cooking

After "good at" or "bad at," use a gerund (running) or a noun (math), not the base verb.

  2. not skilled I am making cakes.
  3. skilled He's math.
  4. not skilled They are cooking.
  5. skilled We're teamwork.
  6. not skilled I'm drawing.
  7. skilled, gerund needed She's good .
  8. skilled He's chess.

Units 46.1, 46.4

Modifying adverbs

Use words like quite, very, and really before an adverb or adjective to change its strength. "Quite" is weaker; "very" and "really" are stronger.

I can ski quite well. She can ski very well. She can ski really well. He is quite tired.

quite / very / really

WordStrengthExample
quitemoderatequite well
verystrongvery well
reallystrongreally well

"Quite" can also mean "completely" before certain adjectives, like "quite right" or "quite finished."

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. moderate strength I can ski well.
  2. strong She can ski well.
  3. strong, spoken style She can ski well.
  4. moderate strength He is tired.
  5. strong They are happy.
  6. strong, spoken style I'm excited about the trip.
  7. moderate strength The movie was good.
  8. strong This coffee is hot.

And Noah, your technique is very smooth." Ana smiles, feeling really proud of her progress.

Units 47.1, 47.7

"I would like" and "I want"

Use want to express a direct desire. Use would like (I'd like) for a more polite way of expressing a desire. Both are followed by "to" plus the base verb.

He wants to write a book. I would like to climb a mountain. I'd like a coffee, please. She wants to travel.

want vs. would like

PhraseToneExample
wantdirectI want to travel.
would like / I'd likepoliteI would like to travel.

"Would like" is often used when ordering food or making polite requests: "I'd like a coffee, please."

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. direct desire, (he) He to write a book.
  2. polite desire, (I) I to climb a mountain.
  3. ordering politely I'd a coffee, please.
  4. direct desire, (she) She to travel.
  5. polite desire, (we) We to help.
  6. direct desire, (they) They to leave early.
  7. contraction of "would" I like to try that dish.
  8. polite desire, (he) He to ask a question.

Reading practice

At the restaurant, the waiter takes orders. "I'd like the soup, please," says Mr. Lee politely. His daughter is more direct. "I want a burger," she says. The waiter smiles. "And for you, sir?" he asks another customer. "I would like the salad, thank you," the man replies. Later, Mr. Lee's son says, "I want dessert!" His father laughs. "You should say, I would like dessert, please." The boy nods. "I'd like dessert, please," he says politely.

Check your understanding

1. Mr. Lee orders the soup.

2. His daughter says "I would like a burger."

3. The other customer orders the salad.

4. Mr. Lee's son says "I want dessert" at first.

5. The boy never learns to be more polite.

Units 48.2, 48.3

"Really" and "quite"

Use really like to show strong enthusiasm. Use quite like to show moderate interest.

I love music. I'd really like to study it this term. I like biology. I'd quite like to study it next year. I'd really like to visit Japan. I'd quite like to try sushi.

would really like / would quite like

PhraseStrengthExample
would really likestrong enthusiasmI'd really like to go.
would quite likemoderate interestI'd quite like to go.

Use "would really like" when you feel strongly, and "would quite like" when your interest is more moderate.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. strong enthusiasm I love music. I'd like to study it this term.
  2. moderate interest I like biology. I'd like to study it next year.
  3. strong enthusiasm I'd like to visit Japan.
  4. moderate interest I'd like to try sushi.
  5. strong enthusiasm She'd like to meet you.
  6. moderate interest He'd like to join the team.
  7. strong enthusiasm We'd like to see that movie.
  8. moderate interest They'd like to visit the museum.

I'd quite like to try engineering." A counselor overhears them. "It's great that you both have interests," she says. "Emma, since you feel so strongly, you'd really like a program focused on art. 5. The counselor discourages both students.

Units 48.7, 48.8

The zero article

Use no article before certain nouns when talking about a general place or institution in its typical function, like "school," "work," "home," or "church." Add the when referring to the specific building.

My daughter goes to school now. He goes to work every day. I go home at six. We go to church on Sundays.

Zero article vs. "the"

UseArticleExample
General functionzero articlego to school
Specific buildingthego to the school (to pick someone up)

"Go to the school" (with "the") usually means visiting the building itself, not attending as a student.

Grammar practice: complete each sentence

  1. general function My daughter goes to now.
  2. general function He goes to every day.
  3. general function I go at six.
  4. general function We go to on Sundays.
  5. specific building I need to go to to pick up some papers.
  6. general function She's at right now.
  7. general function They go to by train.
  8. general function He's at studying.
